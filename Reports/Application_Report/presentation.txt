Slide 1: The Bottleneck in Quadruped State Estimation

Good morning, everyone. Today, we are presenting a novel approach to state estimation for quadruped robots, focusing on environments where the ground itself is unreliable. With the increasing use of quadruped robots in more demanding scenarios, ensuring accurate and stable state estimation in complex environments has become particularly important.  Traditional state estimation algorithms, particularly those relying on multi-sensor fusion, often face significant challenges on non-stationary terrains. When a robot traverses mud, gravel, or wet grass, it encounters foot-end slippage and unstable contact.

In traditional kinematics, the assumption is that a stance foot has zero velocity. When slippage occurs, this assumption is violated, introducing non-Gaussian errors that accumulate over time. This leads to unbounded drift in the robot's state estimation.

This paper solves this bottleneck by introducing an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter, or InEKF. Rather than treating foot-end slippage as random noise, the authors explicitly model it as a deviation term of the body's velocity. By tracking this velocity deviation, the filter can actively estimate and reduce the drift caused by slippage. To accomplish this, they engineered a Right-Invariant EKF that fuses both foot-end velocity and position observations to correct the state in real-time.

Instead of a standard vector, the state variables here form a matrix Lie group in the special euclidean space with 7 attached vectors in 3 physical dimensions -- SE7(3). The core innovation of this paper lies within the final term: the velocity bias. This is the disturbance observer. By appending this drift velocity bias directly into the state equations, the algorithm is forced to continually estimate the slippage speed alongside normal movements.

As the robot moves, the state evolves deterministically using a differential equation driven by the IMU inputs, specifically the gyroscope and accelerometer data.

To track the inherent drift in the IMU data, the growing uncertainty is propagated using the Riccati equation, here notated as equation 3. The filter propagates the state covariance matrix P_t using this equation.

One must keep in mind that in non-stationary terrain, one cannot blindly trust foot kinematics. To handle this, the filter computes a stable contact probability, P_k. As shown in equation 4, this is a logistic regression model with parameters beta1 and beta2, and based on the ground reaction force fz, estimated from joint torques. Evident in this equation, if a leg is pushing firmly against the ground, the probability approaches 1. If it is slipping or hovering, it drops toward 0.

Using this contact probability model, we can combine the basic velocities generated by each leg to obtain the body’s velocity and use it as an observation of the body velocity. 

A threshold is established; here the authors only consider legs that have a contact probability of greater than 50%. This forms the trusted set of feet C. Any foot below that threshold is completely ignored.

Slide 5: Conclusions and Future Trajectory

In conclusion, this paper fundamentally shifts how we handle unstable terrain.

By explicitly modeling foot-end slippage as a velocity deviation term, the proposed InEKF significantly reduces unbounded position drift. Furthermore, by fusing dual kinematic observations with a GRF-based contact probability model, the authors created a highly robust proprioceptive state estimator that thrives even when external sensors fail.

Looking forward, there are several exciting trajectories for this research. First, investigating the fusion of this highly stable proprioceptive baseline with exteroceptive sensors, like vision cameras and LiDAR, to further enhance mapping capabilities. Second, adapting this filter to handle even more extreme terrains with rapidly changing surface properties. Finally, optimizing the computational efficiency of these massive matrix operations to achieve even lower-latency real-time state estimation.
